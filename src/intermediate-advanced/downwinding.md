Downwinding is the moment wing foiling stops feeling like a session in one spot and starts feeling like a journey. Instead of riding back and forth in front of a beach, you point downwind and link one bump of wind swell to the next — pumping just enough to stay connected, gliding the rest — covering real distance on nothing but wind and water. It's one of the most addictive things you can do on a foil, and it's genuinely different from anything you've done up to this point. Here's how to get into it.

## Why Downwind

A normal session is a loop: you ride out, you ride back, you're never far from where you started. A downwind run is point-to-point — you start upwind, finish somewhere downwind of that, and someone (or something) has to get you back. That one-way structure is exactly what makes it special: with the wind always at your back and a trail of bumps to link, you can cover miles with a fraction of the pumping effort a flat-water session takes once you get the timing right.

## Gear for Downwinding

You don't need a whole new quiver to try this, but a few things matter more here than they do close to shore.

**Board.** A long, narrow board is harder to control when you're first learning to link bumps — a mid-length board with moderate volume gives you more stability while you're still finding your feet, without dragging so much that you lose glide.

**Foil.** A higher-aspect-ratio front wing glides longer between pumps, which is exactly what you want when you're trying to connect swell trains instead of constantly repowering. The tradeoff: a stockier, lower-aspect foil turns more easily but won't hold speed as long. Most riders start on something in between rather than jumping straight to a full high-aspect race-style foil.

**Wing.** Size for the wind you'll actually be riding in, same as always — but lean smaller rather than bigger. A wing that's too large gets genuinely hard to manage once you're moving at downwind speed, while a smaller wing stays maneuverable and lets you focus on the swell instead of fighting the wing.

## Reading and Linking Bumps

This is the actual skill, and it's different from anything in flat-water riding.

Don't ride straight into a swell — approach each bump at roughly a 30–45° angle rather than head-on. Hitting it dead straight kills your glide; the angle lets you use the bump's energy instead of just crashing through it.

Your pumping rhythm should sync with the bumps themselves, not run on its own separate cadence: compress as you come down the back of one bump, drive hard through the trough, extend up and forward into the next one — compress, explode, glide, repeat. Keep pressure light on your front foot when you're in a trough (so the nose doesn't dig in) and more weight on your back foot as you steer and set up for the next bump.

None of this clicks immediately. Expect your first several downwind attempts to feel like a lot of pumping and not much gliding — the timing comes with reps, not with reading about it.

## Planning a Safe Run

A downwind run isn't a normal session, and it shouldn't be treated like one.

- **It's point-to-point, so plan the shuttle first.** Someone needs to be at the finish to pick you and your gear up, or you need a car already staged there. Don't figure this out after you've already launched.
- **Confirm the wind will hold for your whole route, not just at launch.** A run that starts in solid wind and dies halfway leaves you stranded, exhausted, a long way from shore. Check the forecast for the full duration and route, not just the moment you check the wind before rigging up.
- **Never go alone on a real offshore run.** Go with at least one other rider, and tell someone on land your planned route and rough return time regardless.
- **Carry more than you think you need for longer or more exposed runs:** a leash, a way to signal for help if your phone isn't reachable, and something that keeps you visible on the water. The gear list scales with how far offshore and how remote the run is — a short coastal hop between two beaches needs a lot less than an open-water point-to-point.

## Where to Start

Don't make your first downwinder a serious offshore mission. Pick a short run close to shore with an easy bail-out option the whole way — somewhere you could self-rescue to land at almost any point along the route if the wind dies or you get tired. Get the bump-linking and the logistics dialed in on something forgiving before you plan anything longer or more exposed. Check the [Where to Ride](/where-to-ride/) map for spots known for good downwind runs, and look for other downwinders in the area — this is a discipline that's genuinely safer and more fun with company.
