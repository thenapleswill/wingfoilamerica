Parawinging is one of the fastest-growing offshoots of wing foiling, and it solves a problem every wing foiler eventually runs into: the wing is great for getting you up and powered, but it's one more thing to manage the moment you actually want to ride a wave or link swell downwind. A parawing is a soft, ultralight, packable wing — no pump, a fraction of the weight of a normal wing — that you use to launch and get up on foil, then fold and stash on your body once you're riding. From there you're riding hands-free: both hands free for the board, the wave, the bumps, exactly like foiling without a wing at all, except you've still got it stashed and ready to redeploy the moment you need power again.

## What Makes It Different From Wing Foiling

A standard wing is inflatable — it needs a pump before every session, weighs several kilograms, and you're holding it the entire time you're riding. A parawing is a single piece of ultralight fabric, closer to paraglider material than a normal wing, weighing a fraction as much and packing down into a bag you could fit in a backpack. There's no pump, no boom, and once you're up and moving, you fold it down and stow it — most riders use a waist pack or stash belt — so it's out of the way entirely until you want it back.

This isn't a replacement for your normal wing setup. It's a specialized tool for a specific job: getting up on foil efficiently, then getting the wing out of your hands so you can actually surf the bump or the wave you're riding. Most people who get into parawinging are already competent wing foilers looking to add this to their existing quiver, not beginners picking it as a first wing.

## Not a Beginner Discipline

This is worth saying plainly: parawinging assumes you can already foil. The water starts are more athletic than a standard wing foiling start — a lot of the technique comes from pumping the board and foil yourself rather than leaning on the wing to power you up, since a parawing simply doesn't have the same continuous pull a standard wing does. If you're still building basic foiling skills, get comfortable wing foiling (or downwind/kite foiling) first. Most experienced parawingers recommend having real hours of wing foiling under you — genuinely dozens, not a handful of sessions — before adding this to the mix.

## Gear for Parawinging

**The parawing itself.** Single-skin parawings are lighter and pack down smaller, which makes them easier to stash mid-session — a good starting choice. Double-skin models hold more shape and drive better upwind, at the cost of a bit more bulk. Size up from what you'd ride on a normal wing — roughly half a meter larger is the typical rule of thumb, since a parawing doesn't have the same continuous power delivery a standard wing does.

**Board.** A longer, higher-volume board than you'd normally ride helps enormously here, especially while you're learning — something in the range of 110-120% of your body weight in liters is a common starting point. The extra stability and glide make the athletic water start much more manageable.

**Foil.** Go one size up from your usual wing-foiling front wing. A stable, forgiving foil matters more here than in standard wing foiling, since you won't always have the wing's pull to bail you out mid-transition.

**Stash system.** A waist pack or dedicated stash belt to hold the folded wing while you're riding hands-free — this is what makes the whole discipline work, not an optional accessory.

**Safety gear.** A board leash is non-negotiable. Many riders also use a harness line for upwind stretches, since parawings can be physically demanding to hold and control by hand alone for long periods. Opinions are split on using a leash for the wing itself, but especially while learning, it's worth having — a lost parawing can sink, tangle, or drift, and it's harder to recover than a normal wing.

## Learning the Basics

Start on land. Ground-handling the parawing — getting a feel for how it flies, powers, and depowers without a boom or rigid handles — translates directly to the water and costs you nothing to practice. Once you're in the water, expect the launch and initial pump-up to foil to feel noticeably more physical than a standard wing foiling start; this is normal, and it's the main reason this isn't a first-wing discipline. Once you're up and stable, practice folding and stashing the wing in calm, controlled conditions before you try to do it while actually riding a wave or a bump — get the mechanics down separately from the riding itself.

## Where Parawinging Actually Shines

Downwind runs and wave riding are the two things a parawing is genuinely built for. On a downwind run, it lets you get up and moving efficiently, then stash it and link bumps hands-free the same way you would on a pure downwind foil session — see the [Downwinding](/intermediate-advanced/downwinding/) page for the planning and safety fundamentals of a point-to-point run, since all of that still applies here. Wave riding is the other obvious fit: paddle-in style, wing-assisted takeoffs, then a completely wing-free ride down the face once you're stashed and moving.

## Where to Start

Don't make your first parawing session a downwind mission or a real wave day. Practice ground-handling first, then find calm, reliable, moderate wind close to shore and focus purely on the mechanics: launching, getting up on foil, and stowing/redeploying the wing, before you ask it to do anything more demanding. Going with someone who already parawings, or at least someone experienced in wing foiling generally, makes the learning curve a lot less frustrating.
