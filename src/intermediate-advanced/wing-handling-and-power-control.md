Every other topic in this section — jumping, downwinding, wave riding, racing — quietly assumes one thing underneath it: that you can control how much power your wing is putting out at any given moment, quickly and without thinking about it. That control comes almost entirely from where your hands are and how you angle the wing to the wind, not from the wing size you bought. Two riders on the same wing in the same wind can have completely different sessions depending on how well they manage this.

## Hand Position Is the Power Dial

**Front hand**, near the leading edge tip, stays relatively fixed — it's mostly what steers the wing up and down and controls direction.

**Back hand**, on the central strut, is your power dial. Slide it back toward the trailing edge and power builds immediately; slide it forward toward the center and it drops off. This should become a small, near-constant, almost unconscious adjustment as you ride — not something you stop to think about, more like breathing.

**Central handle** is the wing's balance point — used on land, in light wind, or any time you want to hold the wing with minimal power and drag.

## Reading the Wing's Angle

Roughly **45° to the horizon** is the everyday cruising position — steady power for holding speed and staying up on foil.

**Near-vertical, arms spread wide** is maximum power — useful in light wind to get moving and up on foil, or any time you deliberately need a burst.

**Lifted flat overhead** is minimum power — it lets a gust pass over you instead of loading into the wing, and can actually help lift your body a little as the wind spills past above you. This is the move to reach for the instant a gust hits harder than expected.

## Depowering Fast, Without Letting Go

The instinct in a scary gust is to let go of the wing entirely — resist that where you can. Lifting the wing overhead sheds power in a fraction of a second while keeping you connected to your gear, which matters both for control and because a fully released wing can become its own hazard, to you or to someone else nearby. Practice this move deliberately on a calm day, before you actually need it in a real gust — it should be reflexive, not something you're figuring out for the first time under pressure.

## Where to Start

Practice away from full sessions first. Standing on the beach or in shin-deep water, walk through all three hand positions and the overhead depower move slowly, without worrying about the board or foil at all — just get your hands used to where they need to go. Then bring it into light-wind sessions before pushing it in gustier conditions or on bigger wings, where the consequences of a slow reaction are higher. This is the one skill worth deliberately over-practicing, because every other topic in this section assumes you already have it.
