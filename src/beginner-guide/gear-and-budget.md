## What Gear Do You Actually Need?

Ask five different people what wing you should buy and you'll get five different answers, and five different opinions on the board and foil to go with it. That's not because the sport is complicated — it's because there's no single right setup. The gear that gets one rider planing is the wrong call for someone twenty pounds lighter, riding a gustier beach, or picking this up for the first time versus their second season.

That gap between "what works for someone" and "what works for you" is the single biggest thing that trips people up before they've even gotten wet. New riders face a wall of wing sizes, board volumes, and foil configurations, get pulled in three directions by well-meaning advice, and either freeze up or spend real money on a setup that fights them from day one. An oversized foil, a board with too little volume to find your feet on, a wing sized for someone else's weight and wind — these aren't rare mistakes, they're the default outcome of guessing.

The calculator below exists to take the guessing out of it. Enter your body weight, experience level, the wind you actually ride in, and roughly what you want to spend, and it hands back a starting-point recommendation: a wing size, board volume, foil size, and mast length, plus a ballpark total cost. Treat it as a well-informed first draft, not a verdict — it can't feel the water or know your local sandbar, so use the output as the start of a conversation with a shop or instructor, not the last word.

### What's actually driving those numbers

However you use the calculator, it's worth understanding why those four inputs are the ones that matter.

**Body weight** sets the baseline for nearly everything else — how much lift you need from the wing and foil, and how much board volume you need to float comfortably before you're up on foil.

**Experience level** matters because skill changes what "manageable" means. A beginner needs more stability and forgiveness built into every piece of gear; a rider with a season or two under them can handle smaller, twitchier equipment that rewards precision instead of punishing it.

**Typical local wind** decides how much power the wing needs to generate. A wing sized for a gusty 25-knot afternoon will be miserable on a light 12-knot day and vice versa — gear has to match the wind you'll actually ride, not the wind on your best day ever.

**Budget** shapes what's realistic to buy new versus used, and whether you spread money across pieces or concentrate it into one solid setup. It doesn't change the physics, but it changes the plan.

One more reality worth expecting early: almost nobody rides one wing size forever. As you progress, you'll likely build a small "quiver" of a couple of wing sizes for different wind strengths, since no single wing covers a truly light day and a truly windy one equally well. Boards and foils change less often — the wing is the piece most riders end up doubling up on.

If you're a total beginner, size your board up slightly beyond what feels minimally necessary. Extra volume buys stability while you're finding your balance, and that stability is what lets you log time on the water instead of fighting to stay on the board. Resist buying "advanced" gear on day one to save a step later — smaller, twitchier equipment is a reward you earn with hours, not a shortcut to them. Let your gear evolve alongside your skill.

## New vs. Used Gear & Realistic Budgets

Gear is the first real decision point in this sport, and it's an expensive one no matter which direction you go. Before you spend anything, it helps to separate two questions that get tangled together: should you buy new or used, and how much should you actually expect to spend? Neither answer is universal, but there's a sensible default for most beginners — and it's not the one gear shops are incentivized to tell you.

### Why used gear makes sense for beginners

Wing foiling has a steep, fast-moving learning curve. The wing size, board volume, and foil setup that feel right in month one are rarely what you'll want by month six. You'll develop preferences — smaller vs. larger wings, more freeride vs. more performance-oriented foils, front-wing shapes suited to your local conditions — that you can't predict before you have them. That makes over-investing in a brand-new, top-of-the-line setup on day one a questionable use of money, not just a matter of taste.

It's also a good time to be buying used. The wing foil market has grown substantially over the past several years, and the secondhand market has grown right along with it — there are now meaningfully more used wings, boards, and foils in circulation than even a couple of seasons ago, which means better selection and less of the "take whatever's available" compromise that used gear shopping used to involve in a smaller, newer sport.

There's also a straightforward economic case: like most sports equipment, wing foil gear depreciates fastest in its first year or two. A wing or foil that's a season or two old has often lost a significant chunk of its original price while still having plenty of usable life left. Buying from someone who's upgrading — not offloading something broken — is frequently a genuinely good value, not merely a compromise.

### What to inspect on used gear

Buying used only works out if you know what you're looking at. A little bit of inspection effort up front can save you from a wing that won't hold air or a foil mast with a hidden crack.

**Wings.** Check the leading edge and bladder for soft spots, slow leaks, or signs of prior repair (patched fabric, re-glued seams) — inflate it and leave it for 20–30 minutes to see if it holds pressure. Look closely at the canopy fabric, especially near the wingtips and center strut, for UV degradation — fading, chalkiness, brittleness. Wings are particularly vulnerable to sun damage when left inflated and unfolded on the beach between sessions, so ask the seller directly how it was stored. Finally, check the inflation valve for cracks or a worn seal — a valve that doesn't seal properly can ruin an otherwise good wing.

**Boards.** Look over the deck and rails for dings, paying particular attention to the area around the mast track or foil mount, since that's the highest-stress zone on the board and the most common place for hairline cracks to start. Press gently on the deck in a grid pattern to feel for soft spots that indicate delamination between the outer skin and the core. A few cosmetic dings that have been properly sealed are usually fine; anything soft or spongy is a bigger red flag.

**Foils.** Inspect the mast closely for stress cracks or corrosion, particularly if it's aluminum — aluminum masts are more prone to corrosion than carbon, especially if the foil has spent time in saltwater without being rinsed. Check the front wing's leading edge for dings or impact damage, since that area affects both performance and structural integrity. Finally, wiggle the connections between the fuselage, mast, and wings to check for play or wobble — a foil with loose tolerances at these joints feels unstable on the water, and that wear isn't always cheap to fix.

### Where people actually buy and sell

The used wing foil market lives in a few predictable places. General marketplace platforms with local buy-and-sell groups are a common first stop, since they let you see gear in person before paying. Gear-specific forums and secondhand marketplaces built around watersports or foiling specifically are another good source, often with sellers who are more transparent about condition. Local shops that take gear on consignment are worth checking too — the shop has usually given the equipment at least a basic once-over, and you get a local point of contact if something's wrong. Regional wing foil groups and communities (Facebook groups, local club channels) are also worth watching, since gear sold within a community tends to come with a more honest history attached.

### The case for buying new

Used isn't automatically the right call for everyone, and there are real reasons to buy new even as a beginner. New gear comes with a warranty — foils and wings do occasionally fail, and a manufacturer defect covered under warranty is a very different experience than being stuck with a used piece that breaks with no recourse. New gear also gives you the latest safety features, which have moved fast in this sport: modern leash systems and wing valve/deflation designs are meaningfully safer than what shipped a few years ago. And buying new means no unknown history — no wondering whether that soft spot on the board is cosmetic or structural — plus sizing everything exactly to your weight and conditions rather than compromising on whatever's available secondhand nearby.

### A practical recommendation

For a first setup, used or rental gear is usually the smarter starting point. Your preferences and skill level will shift quickly in the first year, and buying new before you know what you actually want is a good way to end up with an expensive wing or board that doesn't fit how you ride six months later. Once you've logged enough sessions to know your preferred riding style — freeride, downwind, wave, or something more performance-oriented — buying new starts to make a lot more sense, because at that point you're buying gear matched to an actual, informed preference rather than a guess.

As a rough starting point, the site's own gear calculator estimates roughly $1,500–$2,700 total for a budget/used-gear package (wing, board, foil set, and accessories combined), $2,950–$4,450 for a mid-range mix of new and value picks, and $4,500–$6,800 for a premium, top-of-the-line new setup. These are estimates, not guarantees — actual prices vary by brand, region, and what's available secondhand near you at any given moment — but they're a reasonable frame for what to expect walking in. If you haven't run your own numbers yet, the calculator above will factor in your weight, experience level, and local wind conditions to give you a more personalized estimate.
